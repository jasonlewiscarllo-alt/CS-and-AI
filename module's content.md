Network

A network is like a group of friends who can communicate with one another, share ideas, and exchange things whenever they need to. In the same way, a computer network is a group of two or more devices, such as computers, laptops, mobile phones, tablets, and printers, that are connected to each other so they can share information and resources easily. These devices can communicate and exchange data through cables, wires, or wireless signals such as Wi-Fi. For example, when we send a message to a friend, share photos, print a document from a laptop, or use the internet on our phones, we are using a network. Networks make it easier and faster for people to communicate, work together, and share important information. They also help save time and resources because several devices can use the same internet connection, printer, or files. Networks can be small, such as a network connecting devices in a home or school, or very large, connecting computers across different cities and countries. In simple words, a computer network allows different devices to connect, communicate, and share information and resources with one another.

How the Internet work














In networking, a packet is a small part of a larger piece of information that is sent over the internet. Each packet contains the actual data and a header, which includes important details about the data, such as where it needs to go. The header helps the receiving device understand how to handle the packet.
When we send information over the internet, such as a photo, video, or message, the data is divided into smaller packets. These packets travel through network devices like routers and switches until they reach their destination. Once they arrive, the receiving device puts the packets back together so the information can be viewed or used. This process is similar to building a puzzle, where each piece is sent separately and then joined together to complete the picture.
Sending data in packets also makes the internet more efficient because many devices can send and receive information at the same time. This method is called packet switching. Instead of one connection taking up the whole network, packets can travel through available routes, allowing many people to use the internet simultaneously.
Common Components of a Computer Network
A computer network consists of several important components that work together to help devices communicate, exchange information, and share resources. Each component has a specific role in making sure data travels from one device to another correctly and efficiently.
1. Nodes (Devices)
Nodes are the devices connected to a network that can send, receive, or process data. These devices can include computers, laptops, mobile phones, printers, servers, and other smart devices. Every node plays a role in communicating with other devices or sharing resources over the network.
For example, when you connect your phone and laptop to your home Wi-Fi, both devices become nodes in the network. A printer connected to the same network is also a node because other devices can send documents to it for printing.
2. Links (Connections)
Links are the connections that allow devices in a network to communicate with one another. These connections can be wired or wireless, depending on the type of network and the devices being used.
Wired links use physical cables, such as Ethernet cables, to transfer data between devices. Wireless links use signals, such as Wi-Fi or Bluetooth, to send information without needing a physical cable.
For example, when you connect your laptop to a router using an Ethernet cable, the cable acts as a link. When you connect your phone to the same router through Wi-Fi, the wireless connection acts as a link. Without links, devices would not be able to exchange information.
3. Routers
Routers are networking devices that connect different networks and forward data between them. They examine information in data packets, such as destination IP addresses, to determine where the data should go next.
You can think of a router as a GPS navigation system that helps information find the correct route to its destination. It chooses an appropriate path based on available network routes.
For example, when you open a website on your phone, your home router forwards your request from your home network toward the internet. When the website sends information back, the router helps deliver the data to your device. Routers are commonly used in homes, schools, offices, and large organizations.
4. Switches
Switches are networking devices that connect multiple devices within a local area network (LAN). They help send data to the correct device instead of sending it unnecessarily to every connected device.
A switch mainly uses MAC addresses to identify devices on a local network. When it receives data, it checks the destination MAC address and forwards the data through the appropriate port.
For example, in a school computer lab, several computers and printers may be connected to the same switch. When one computer sends a document to a network printer, the switch forwards the data toward the printer.
Switches are useful in offices, schools, and other places where many devices need to communicate within the same local network.
5. Protocols
Protocols are the rules and standards that devices follow to communicate with one another over a network. They define how data is formatted, transmitted, received, and understood by different devices.Since different devices may use different hardware and software, they need to follow common rules so they can understand the information being exchanged. Protocols work like a common language that helps devices communicate correctly.
Without protocols, devices might not understand the information sent by other devices. Protocols help ensure that communication happens in an organized and reliable way.
Some common network protocols include:
TCP(Transmission Control Protocol):It helps ensure data arrives reliably and in the correct order so that the information is delivered accurately
IP: is the identity of the devices that will send information or receive them it acts similar to a mailbox where the router is the one who leads it to its proper destination at the most efficient route but that will sometimes cause the information to be jumbled up or mismatched that is why TCP is used
HTTP/HTTPS: Protocols used to transfer website information between web browsers and web servers. HTTPS also encrypts the connection to ensure it is secure.
FTP: A protocol used to transfer files between computers over a network.
DNS: A system that translates domain names, such as google.com, into IP addresses that computers use to locate servers.
Ethernet:It connects computers, routers, and other devices to build a local area network (LAN) and link them to the internet. 
For example, when you visit a website, DNS helps your device find the website's server, while TCP/IP helps move data between your device and that server.In conclusion, packets divide information into smaller pieces so it can be sent efficiently, while protocols provide the rules that allow devices to communicate and understand the data they receive. Both are important for making the internet work properly.

Network Threats 
Malware:
Virus: A program that attaches itself to a host file or program, replicates by copying itself, and corrupts, modifies, or deletes data.
Worm: A standalone program that self-replicates and spreads across networks without needing a host file or human interaction.
Trojan Horse: Malicious code disguised as or bundled inside a legitimate, useful piece of software; once installed, it creates backdoors for attackers.
Spyware: Secretive software that monitors user activity (such as keystrokes, screen captures, and browsing history) and transmits the data back to a third party.
Adware: Software that automatically displays unwanted, intrusive pop-up advertisements and may redirect browser traffic.
Ransomware:Software that encrypts a victim's files or locks the hard drive, demanding a financial ransom (often in cryptocurrency) in exchange for the decryption key. 

Cyberattacks:
Brute-Force & Dictionary Attacks: Trying every possible combination of a password or PIN (or using a list of common words) until the correct credentials are found.
Data Interception (Packet Sniffing): Eavesdropping on data as it travels across a wired or wireless network using software or hardware "packet sniffers". 
Denial of Service (DoS) & Distributed Denial of Service (DDoS): Overwhelming a server or network with a massive flood of data requests from a single source (DoS) or a botnet of multiple infected computers (DDoS) so it crashes or becomes unavailable. 
Hacking / Unauthorized Access: Gaining illegal entry into a computer system or network to view, delete, or alter data. 
Phishing: Sending fraudulent emails, SMS (smishing), or phone calls (vishing) that mimic legitimate organizations to trick users into revealing sensitive data or passwords. 
Pharming: Using malicious code or DNS/browser manipulation to silently redirect a user from a legitimate website to a fake replica site designed to steal credentials. 
Social Engineering: Manipulating human psychology (trust, fear, curiosity, or authority) to persuade individuals to break security procedures or reveal confidential information.


System & Network Vulnerabilities:
Weak or Default Passwords: Using easily guessed, short, or factory-default passwords (e.g., 123456, admin).
Unpatched / Outdated Software: Running operating systems or applications missing critical security patches, leaving known vulnerabilities open to exploit.
Misconfigured Access Rights: Setting file permissions or server roles too loosely (e.g., giving standard users administrative privileges).
Removable Media: Using infected USB flash drives, CDs, or external hard drives that bypass network firewalls when plugged into a system.
Common Types of Network Security
1. Firewall: A security system that monitors and controls incoming and outgoing network traffic based on defined rules. It helps block unwanted or unauthorized connections.
2. Encryption: Converts readable information into an encoded form so that unauthorized people cannot easily understand it. HTTPS is an example of technology that uses encryption to protect web communication.
3. Antivirus and Anti-malware: Security software that detects, blocks, and removes malicious programs that could damage devices or steal information.
4. Authentication: Verifies a user's identity before allowing access to a system or network. Examples include passwords, fingerprint scans, and multi-factor authentication (MFA).
5. VPN (Virtual Private Network): Creates an encrypted connection between a device and a VPN endpoint, helping protect data while it travels across an untrusted network.
6. Intrusion Detection and Prevention Systems (IDS/IPS): Monitor network traffic for suspicious activity. An IDS detects and reports potential threats, while an IPS can also block certain malicious traffic.
















Network topology
Network topology the physical and logical arrangement of nodes (such as computers, servers, and routers) and connections (such as cables or wireless links) in a computer network 
Bus Topology

To allow multiple devices to communicate with each other over a single, shared physical medium without needing a complex web of individual wires. 
Pros (Advantages)
Cost-Effective: Requires the least amount of cabling compared to star or mesh topologies.
Simple Design: Very straightforward to set up, understand, and connect new devices to for small networks.
No Extra Power Needed: The passive backbone does not require a central hub, switch, or router to function.
Easy Extension:Adding more nodes is simple by tapping into the main line (as long as network limits aren't exceeded) 
Cons (Disadvantages)
Single Point of Failure: If the main backbone cable breaks or gets damaged anywhere along the line, the entire network goes down.
Poor Scalability: Performance drops quickly as more devices are added due to increased data traffic and collisions.
High Collision Rate: Because all devices share the same wire, simultaneous data transmissions collide and require re-transmission (managed via protocols like CSMA/CD).
Difficult Troubleshooting: Finding a break, loose tap, or faulty terminator along a long cable run is hard and time-consuming.
Low Security: Because every packet is broadcast to every node, any computer on the bus can potentially sniff or intercept traffic.
Point-to-Point

A point-to-point topology is the simplest form of network architecture where a dedicated communication link directly connects two devices (nodes) to each other without any intermediate switches or hubs. 
Pros (Advantages)
Simplicity: Easy to set up, configure, and manage because it only involves two endpoints. 
High Performance & Bandwidth: The entire capacity and speed of the link are reserved exclusively for the two connected devices without sharing or congestion from other nodes. 
Low Latency: Data travels straight from the source to the destination with no routing delays. 
Enhanced Security: Data moves across a private, isolated channel, reducing the risk of interception or unauthorized sniffing compared to shared media. 
Low Initial Hardware Cost: For a single pair of devices, minimal hardware and cabling are required.
Cons (Disadvantages)
Poor Scalability: Adding more devices is difficult and expensive, as every new node requires its own dedicated physical or logical link. 
Single Point of Failure: If the single connecting cable or link fails, all communication between the two devices stops completely. 
High Cost at Scale: Connecting multiple locations or devices requires a separate dedicated line (such as a leased line) for each pair, which becomes very costly. 
Limited Flexibility: The architecture is strictly locked to two endpoints, making it unsuited for multi-user local area networks (LANs). 
Higher Maintenance Overhead: Managing multiple independent point-to-point connections across a larger environment increases administrative and troubleshooting complexity


Star Topology

A star topology is a computer network setup where every node (device) connects independently to a central device, such as a hub, switch, or router 

Pros(Advantages)
Isolated failures: If one cable or device breaks, only that specific node goes down while the rest of the network keeps working normally. 
Easy scalability: You can add or remove devices easily just by plugging them into or unplugging them from the central switch. 
Simple troubleshooting: Finding a broken connection is straightforward because every device has its own dedicated line leading back to the center. 
Better performance: Using a modern switch allows unicast data flow, reducing data collisions and keeping speeds high compared to older shared-media layouts like bus topologies. 
Centralized management: Network administrators can easily monitor traffic, spot issues, and manage devices from the central hub. 
Cons(Disadvantages)
Single point of failure: If the central hub, switch, or router stops working, the entire network goes down. 
Higher cabling cost: Running a separate cable from every single device to a central location requires more wire and installation effort than a linear bus layout. 
Hub/switch bottlenecks: Heavy network traffic passing through the central device can cause slowdowns if the hardware lacks the capacity to handle it. 
Hardware dependency: The overall performance and maximum capacity of the network are completely limited by the specs and reliability of the central device. 


Mesh TopologyPros(Advantages)
High Fault Tolerance: If a single node or cable fails, data automatically reroutes through alternative paths without bringing down the network.
No Single Point of Failure: The lack of a central hub means a localized breakdown does not compromise the entire system.
High Traffic Handling: Dedicated and simultaneous data paths reduce congestion and support heavy data loads.
Scalability: You can add new devices or nodes to the network without disrupting existing communication.
Improved Security and Privacy: Direct point-to-point or encrypted multi-path links make data harder to intercept or compromise. 
Cons(Disadvantages)
High Cost: A full mesh requires an extensive number of cables, ports, and hardware components (N(N-1)/2 connections for N nodes), making it expensive to build. 
Complex Setup and Installation: Designing, configuring, and wiring a full mesh network takes a massive amount of planning and physical labor. 
Difficult Maintenance: Troubleshooting broken links or isolated nodes is time-consuming due to the sheer volume of interconnections. 
High Power Consumption: In a wired or always-on setup, keeping all nodes and redundant paths active demands more power.


Ring Topology

A ring topology is a network setup where each device (node) connects to two other devices, forming a closed, circular loop. 



Pros(Advantages)
No data collisions: Data travels in an orderly sequence using a token-passing method, which prevents packet crashes.
Equal resource access: Every device on the ring gets an identical opportunity to transmit and receive data.
Predictable performance: The network maintains steady data speeds and handles heavy traffic much better than a bus topology.
No central server needed: Devices manage communication independently without relying on a main server.
Lower cabling costs: It requires less physical cable than a star topology because each node only connects to its two immediate neighbors. 
Disadvantages
Single point of failure: In a standard single-ring setup, a broken wire or failed node can bring down the entire network.
Difficult troubleshooting: Locating a fault or break requires checking multiple nodes around the circle.
Higher latency: Data must pass sequentially through every intermediate device between the sender and the receiver.
Disruption during changes: Adding or removing a device traditionally requires stopping the entire network.




Hybrid Topology

A hybrid topology is a computer network structure that combines two or more different basic network topologies—such as star, ring, bus, or mesh—into a single unified system. This Topology is also the most used Topology due to the fact that it combines different topologies strength

Pros(Advantages)
High Flexibility & Customization: You can tailor different sections of the network to match specific department workflows, traffic loads, and physical environments.
Effective Fault Isolation: Problems or failures in one segment are usually contained to that specific section without crashing the entire network.
Enhanced Reliability & Uptime: By merging structures, you can build redundancy where it matters most, keeping critical operations running if a sub-network drops.
Scalability: You can easily add new nodes, devices, or entire network segments (like a new star or ring cluster) without disrupting existing operations.
Optimized Performance: Traffic can be routed efficiently through pathways best suited for heavy data loads. 
Disadvantages
High Cost: Hardware components—such as intelligent hubs, multi-protocol routers, and specialized connectors—are expensive.
Complex Design and Setup: Planning, configuring, and installing a multi-layered hybrid architecture requires advanced network engineering skills.
Heavy Cabling and Infrastructure Requirements: Merging diverse layouts often demands extensive physical cabling, cooling systems, and specialized infrastructure.
Difficult Maintenance and Troubleshooting: Because multiple structures work together, diagnosing the root cause of an issue can span across different protocols and physical layouts.


Tree Topology

Tree topology is a hierarchical network structure that combines elements of star and bus topologies, resembling a tree with a central root node, branches, and leaf nodes 
Root nodes are located at the very top where branch nodes are located in the middle and the leaf node is located at the very bottom
Pros(Advantages)
High Scalability: You can easily add new secondary branches and sub-networks without disrupting the rest of the existing network. 
Effective Fault Isolation: Problems or damages in one lower-level branch are contained locally and do not automatically crash other branches. 
Structured Organization: The hierarchical layout mimics corporate or departmental structures, making management and monitoring much clearer for IT teams. 
Point-to-Point Connections: Individual devices and segments use dedicated wiring, which reduces data collisions and simplifies targeted troubleshooting. 
Good Resource Sharing: Centralized servers and storage devices can be shared efficiently across various segments.
Cons(Disadvantages)
Single Point of Failure: If the main root node or a critical upper-level backbone cable fails, large portions or the entire network goes down. 
High Installation Costs: Requiring extensive cabling, backbone components, and multiple hubs makes initial setup expensive. 
Complex Maintenance: As the hierarchy grows larger and more branches are added, cable management and troubleshooting upstream become difficult. 
Performance Bottlenecks: Heavy traffic flowing from lower branches must pass through higher-level nodes, leading to potential slowdowns during peak usage
Types of computer networks
While similar in their overall objectives, various types of networks fulfill different purposes. Networks today are classified in the broad categories below.








1. Local Area Network (LAN)
Local-area network (LAN)










A LAN is a collection of connected devices in one physical location, such as a home or an office.Which uses the star topology since If a single Ethernet cable fails or a computer crashes, only that individual machine loses connection. The rest of the office keeps working perfectly or adding new employees you simply plug a new cable into the central switch. You don't have to take the network offline to expand it . A LAN can be small or large, ranging from a home network with one user to a large enterprise network with thousands of users and devices. A LAN may include both wired and wireless devices.
Regardless of size, a LAN's particular characteristic is that it connects devices that are in a single, limited area.




2. Wide Area Network (WAN)


A Wide Area Network (WAN) connects computers and smaller networks over large geographical areas, such as different cities, countries, or continents. It allows people and organizations in different locations to communicate and share information. It uses either Mesh or Hybrid topology since it connects distinct cities or countries using multiple redundant paths. If an undersea fiber-optic cable is cut, traffic automatically reroutes through another country. 
For example, a company may have offices in Tokyo, Singapore, and London. A WAN can connect the networks in these offices so employees can access company files and systems from different locations.
The internet is the most well-known example of a WAN because it connects millions of networks around the world. WANs often rely on telecommunications companies and internet service providers (ISPs) to carry data over long distances. Compared with a LAN, a WAN usually requires more complex infrastructure and can be more expensive to maintain.
3. Metropolitan Area Network (MAN)

A Metropolitan Area Network (MAN) connects multiple LANs across a city, town, or large campus. It is larger than a typical LAN but generally covers a smaller geographical area than a WAN. It uses Tree Topology as a tree topology allows a city government to act as the "root," branching down to individual municipal departments, schools, and police stations.
For example, a university with several buildings across a city may use a MAN to connect the computer networks in its library, laboratories, administration offices, and other facilities. This allows students and staff to access shared resources across different locations.
A MAN may use fibre-optic cables or other high-speed communication links to transfer data between locations. It is useful for universities, government offices, hospitals, and businesses that need to connect several buildings within the same metropolitan area.
4. Wireless Networks

A Wireless Network connects devices without requiring physical cables between every device. It uses either a Star or Mesh Topology; a wireless Star allows devices to move freely within range of a single Access Point without needing physical cables while Mesh is used for large places since wireless Mesh nodes are used because they blank canvas a large house or office with Wi-Fi by passing the signal from node to node, bypassing thick walls without requiring you to drill holes for cables. It uses wireless signals, such as radio waves, to allow devices to communicate and exchange information. Wi-Fi and Bluetooth are common examples of wireless technologies.
For example, when you connect your phone, laptop, or tablet to your home Wi-Fi, you can access the internet without plugging an Ethernet cable into each device. Bluetooth also allows devices, such as wireless headphones and speakers, to connect over short distances.
Wireless networks are popular because they provide convenience, flexibility, and mobility. People can move around while staying connected, which makes wireless networks useful in homes, schools, offices, airports, and public places. However, wireless connections can be affected by distance, walls, interference, and security risks if they are not properly protected.
5. Telecommunications Networks

A Telecommunications Network is a system that allows information to be transmitted over distances using electrical signals, radio waves, optical fibre, or other communication technologies. It supports communication through voice calls, video calls, text messages, television, and internet services. It uses Mesh or Hybrid Topology since the global telecom system cannot afford a single point of failure. It uses a massive hybrid mesh so that a localized blackout or severed submarine cable doesn't cut off international phone and internet traffic. 
For example, when you make a phone call to someone in another country, telecommunications networks carry the information between you and the other person. These networks may include telephone lines, mobile base stations, fibre-optic cables, satellites, and switching equipment.
Telecommunications networks are important because they allow people, businesses, and organizations to communicate across long distances. They are used in telephone services, broadcasting, emergency communications, video conferencing, and internet connectivity.
6. Cellular Networks
A Cellular Network is a type of wireless telecommunications network that allows mobile phones and other devices to make calls, send messages, and access the internet. It uses cell towers or base stations to send and receive radio signals from mobile devices. It uses a hybrid topology as it uses both a star and mesh topology as it connects to the tower which is the central hub and all nearby phones would connect to it and acts like a mesh topology as the towers are wired together so if the connection breaks from the previous tower it will connect to the closest tower
For example, when you use mobile data to watch a video while travelling, your phone communicates with a nearby cell tower. As you move from one area to another, the network can transfer your connection to another base station to help you remain connected.
Cellular networks include technologies such as 3G, 4G LTE, and 5G. Each generation has introduced improvements in speed, capacity, and communication capabilities. Cellular networks are especially useful because they allow people to communicate and access online services even when they are away from home or a Wi-Fi network.


7. Social Networks
A Social Network is a group of people or organizations connected through friendships, family relationships, work, or shared interests. In computing, the term often refers to online platforms that allow people to communicate, share content, and build relationships.This uses a hybrid topology and uses both a star and mesh topology again where every person is a node and every follower/following is a connection which creates a huge web and it uses a star topology as they use famous people as a central hub to connect several people who follow them at the same time
Examples include Facebook, Instagram, LinkedIn, and other social media platforms. Users can create profiles, post photos, exchange messages, share videos, and interact with other people.
Social networks make it easier for people to stay in touch regardless of their geographical location. They are also useful for businesses that want to promote products, for professionals who want to develop career connections, and for communities that want to share information. However, users should be careful about privacy, misinformation, and the personal information they share online.
Unlike LAN and WAN, which classify networks by their geographical coverage, a social network describes connections between people rather than a particular type of physical computer network.
8. Neural Networks
A Neural Network is a type of computing model inspired by the way the human brain processes information. It consists of interconnected artificial neurons that process data and learn patterns from examples. It uses a hybrid topology which uses the tree and mesh topology and uses the tree topology where it uses the tree topology for organizing and processing data step by step from the root to the branch then to the leaf and uses mesh topology for thinking and pattern recognition by attempting to piece a shredded image together and figures it out through connecting them.
For example, a neural network can be trained to recognize faces in photographs, identify spoken words, detect spam emails, or help an artificial intelligence system understand language.
Neural networks learn by processing training data and adjusting the connections between their artificial neurons. After training, they can use the patterns they have learned to make predictions or classify new information. They are widely used in artificial intelligence, image recognition, speech recognition, medical research, and recommendation systems.


Direction of Data Flow and Methods of Synchronizatio

1. Simplex (One-way)


Data travels in only one direction.
The sender can send data, but the receiver cannot send data back through that communication channel.
Example: Traditional TV broadcasting and a computer keyboard sending input to a computer.






2. Half-Duplex Mode


Data can travel in both directions, but not at the same time.
One device sends data while the other receives it. Then they can switch roles.
Example: Walkie-talkies.
3. Full-Duplex Mode


Data travels in both directions at the same time.
Both devices can send and receive data simultaneously.
Example: A telephone conversation.





Methods of data transmission
Data transmission is the process of sending data from one device to another. There are two main types: parallel transmission and serial transmission.


1. Parallel Transmission

Parallel Transmission sends many bits of data at the same time from one computer to another. It's faster than Serial and works well for short distances. It fits well with how computers are built inside, as they use parallel systems. Computers and other devices can easily use Parallel Transmission.
It's easy to set up and fix problems with Parallel Transmission. It uses one cable with a 25-pin port. This port has 17 signal lines and 8 ground lines. The 17 lines are split into 4 lines to start the data transfer, 5 lines to talk and report errors, and 8 lines to move the data.
2. Serial Transmission


In Serial transmission, data is sent bit by bit from one computer to another using a two-way path. Each bit has its own timing. In Serial Transmission, 8 bits are sent at a time, with extra bits at the start and end (called Parity bits), which are 0 and 1. This method uses special cables to send data over long distances. The data is sent in order. It uses a 9-pin cable shaped like a D to connect data in a series. 

Methods of Synchronization in Data Communication
Synchronization is the process of coordinating the sender and receiver so that the receiver can identify when data starts, how to read it, and when it ends.
There are three methods of synchronization commonly discussed in data communication.
1. Asynchronous Transmission

Asynchronous processing is the opposite of synchronous processing, as the client does not have to wait for a response after a request is made, and can continue other forms of processing. This process is referred to as non-blocking because the execution thread of the client is not blocked after the request is made. This allows systems to scale better as more work can be done in a given amount of time. 



2. Synchronous 


Synchronous processing is the traditional way of processing in client-server communication. A client sends a request to a server and waits for the server to complete the job and send a response before the client can continue doing any other work. This process is often referred to as blocking (i.e. the client is blocked from doing any other work until a response is received).
